A plan that fits their age

A 7-year-old and a 13-year-old should not train the same way. You enter a birth year and the sessions match it. The Parent tab explains why.

  • Ages 6 to 8: learn to move Short sessions, starting at 20 minutes, that teach how to run, jump, land, throw and catch. Landing quietly counts as much as jumping far. The hardest drills wait.
  • Ages 9 to 11: add speed and strength The full 30-minute session, with harder jumps and short, all-out sprints. Before the teenage years, strength comes mostly from the body learning to use the muscle it has, so push-ups, squats and hangs done well are enough.
  • Ages 12 to 14: learn the strength moves Speed, power and footwork days of 45 minutes. A control warm-up first, then stopping, landing and cutting at speed, and the squat, hinge, push, pull and carry taught one at a time. A band and a light medicine ball are optional.

Kids grow at different rates, so the ages are a guide. A parent sets the weekday time and decides when a level goes up. Athletes of 15 to 18 can use the app, but a plan written for high school is still to come.

The climb

For kids who play more than one sport

Pediatric guidance is to play more than one sport through childhood and to put off choosing just one until the later teens. Athletes who specialize early have more overuse injuries and are more likely to burn out. And the best athletes tend to have played more sports as kids, not fewer: a review of 51 studies covering more than 6,000 athletes found that athletes who reached international level did more multi-sport practice growing up, and started their main sport later, than athletes who peaked at national level.

  • Three of the four weekday sessions are shared. Speed, power and footwork are the same for football, basketball, baseball, softball, hockey and soccer. Only the skill session changes with the sport.
  • Change sport with the season. The speed, power and footwork levels carry over, and every finished session stays on the climb. The skill track starts at Rookie in the new sport.
  • Sunday is play day. A different sport is the first choice on the list, and a game or a team practice counts as the session.
  • One membership covers the family, whatever each athlete plays.

Where this comes from: the American Academy of Pediatrics' reports on sports specialization (2016), overuse injuries and burnout (2024) and resistance training for children (2020), a 2015 study of 1,190 young athletes, and a 2021 review of what international-level athletes did as children. None of them has reviewed or endorsed the app. This is a general training plan and not medical advice.
